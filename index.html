Pickle's Story Time: How West Hollywood's Drag Laureate Brings Education and Drag Together

Reported & Photographed by Clayah Wei / Hong Kong Baptist University

About a dozen children gathered around Pickle on the floor of the Academy Museum Store in Los Angeles on June 13, 2026. Most sat beside their mothers as she held a copy of Lil Miss Hot Mess’s The Hips on the Drag Queen Go Swish, Swish, Swish.

She began with an altered version of "The Wheels on the Bus." "The shoulders on the drag queen go shimmy, shimmy, shimmy," Pickle sang, shrugging her shoulders. Several children stood and copied her, shouting the final "shimmy" back at her.

Two days later, on June 15, 2026, the West Hollywood City Council honored Pickle and passed the crown to her successor, Odious Ari, the city's first drag king. City officials said Pickle had not only represented drag in West Hollywood, but had defined the role during her three years in it. The June 13 session was among the last she led as the city's inaugural Drag Laureate.

Who Is Pickle, and What Is Drag Story Hour

Pickle, whose legal name is Joseph Marcellus Faragher, is a drag queen and the Los Angeles organizer of Drag Story Hour. She uses she/her pronouns and is also a middle school teacher, though she asked that the school's name not be published to protect student privacy. She leads Drag Story Hour in different public spaces across Los Angeles nearly every week.

Drag Story Hour was founded in San Francisco in 2015 by writer Michelle Tea and RADAR Productions, with organizers Julián Delgado Lopera and Virgie Tovar playing leading roles in its early development. The program uses drag as a storytelling medium to introduce audiences to LGBTQ communities and to encourage self-acceptance and understanding of gender expression and identity through LGBTQIA+ stories.

Pickle began doing drag as a student at Hamilton High, when she appeared in a school production of A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum and played a character who gets into drag. In an interview with LAist, she described the moment she put on the wig, lipstick and dress: "Something was kind of awakened where I was like, 'Oh, this works.'" She continued exploring drag while at Sarah Lawrence College in New York.

How Pickle Builds a Story Hour

Pickle said the sessions are aimed mostly at young children and teenagers, but everyone is welcome. When selecting books, she looks for work by LGBTQ creators or stories that introduce LGBTQ experiences in an accessible way.

One example is The Hips on the Drag Queen Go Swish, Swish, Swish by Lil Miss Hot Mess, a drag queen, author, storyteller and board member of Drag Story Hour. Another is Feminist Baby by Loryn Brantz, which introduces ideas of equality and autonomy in a playful way. She also uses books like This Day in June by Gayle E. Pitman, which depicts a Pride Parade.

After the reading, Pickle builds in movement and interaction. She leads songs, dances and small games that connect back to the book, so children can respond physically to what they just heard.

In an earlier Beverly Press interview, Pickle described the difficulty of explaining her role. "I just never came up with a good enough answer," she said. "I get to work with children, and sometimes I feel very silly, because it's like, 'Well, what is this? I'm the only one, really.' But I always tell my friends and people ... In 60 years, I'll be so proud to have been this person."

The Drag Laureate Role

A laureate is typically someone honored for achievement in the arts or sciences. Pickle is the inaugural Drag Laureate of the City of West Hollywood, a paid civic role created in 2023. She was selected in June 2023 through an application process run by the city's Arts and Cultural Affairs Commission. Her original term was set to run from July 1, 2023, to July 1, 2025, but the city extended it for a third year, through July 1, 2026. The city provides a $12,500 annual award and a $2,500 annual stipend for city drag events, totaling $15,000 per year.

“Pickle was selected as the drag laureate to serve a two-year term, but we couldn't let Pickle go, so we extended it to three,” Mayor John Heilman said.

The position's duties include raising the presence and appreciation of drag culture and art in West Hollywood, fostering partnerships with local businesses and community organizations, celebrating the city's spirit, and inspiring emerging drag artists through drag history. West Hollywood Councilmember Lauren Meister, who helped draft the original Drag Laureate proposal, told the Los Angeles Times: "The LGBTQ community has such a history with the city. We wanted the drag laureate to be an ambassador to West Hollywood businesses but also promote art and culture. We wanted to do something that could bring some levity back to our city and keep things edgy."

During her term, Pickle appeared at WeHo Pride, the West Hollywood Halloween Carnaval and National Night Out. She created quarterly drag roundtables at Plummer Park Community Center to connect drag performers of all backgrounds with resources, organized free sewing workshops and drag story hours, participated in Harvey Milk Day events, hosted a drag pageant honoring the late LGBTQ rights activist José Julio Sarria, and hosted a celebration of West Hollywood's 39th anniversary.

On Pride Parade day itself, June 14, 2026, Pickle appeared as a guest performer at Pride Night at the Aquarium of the Pacific in Long Beach, an event organized with the LGBTQ Center Long Beach.

Not Always Welcomed

Drag Story Hour has not always been welcomed. On October 25, 2023, Pickle arrived at the San Fernando Public Library for a scheduled Drag Story Hour and found the entrance blocked by protesters and barricades. She told ABC7 that she hoped someone would be held accountable and that people would understand the importance of Drag Story Hour.

“It is about self-expression,” said Pickle. “These programs are completely G-rated, and if they don't want to bring their children for whatever reason, that is their right. It is not their right to determine whether or not public programs are offered through a public library.”

At the Academy Museum of Motion Pictures, a little girl looked up at Pickle, tall and glamorous, and turned to her mother sitting behind her.

"Is she a queen?" she whispered.

Pickle heard this and bent down.

"Yes," she said. "My dear little princess!"

The Invisible Gay City: Los Angeles LGBTQ History Is Disappearing

"You can see it now," Eric A. Curry said, pointing across the street. "That building no longer exists."

Following his gesture, the present-day site of 232 S. Hill St. in Downtown Los Angeles holds no monument or historic building, only an empty parking lot.

This history is disappearing fast: buildings are torn down, records go missing, and the people who lived it are dying. Once they are gone, there may be no one left to say what happened here — which is why it needs to be told now.

Eric A. Curry, a historical tour guide with California Queer Adventures, led the tour that day, joined only by the reporters and his brother.

Eric said LGBTQ activists he had interviewed warned him: "Queer history here in the United States is being erased at a very, very quick pace."

420 N Main St. | Merced Theatre and hidden social life

The tour's first stop was the Merced Theatre at 420 N Main St. The Los Angeles Conservancy says the building was constructed in 1870, opened on January 30, 1871, and closed in 1877; it also notes that, at the turn of the twentieth century, the theater hosted masked balls that offered LGBTQ people a relatively safe gathering place, and that beginning in 1897 it was used as a lodging house for queer men.

According to Chris Cruse's Queer Map project, the theater was later converted into a covert gay lodging house by "outrageous queen" Frederick Purssord.

Standing before the theater's closed doors, Eric explained why those private spaces mattered.

“I really can't emphasize enough how important these private events were,” he said.

Entry to these gatherings often involved some degree of screening, making them among the few private social spaces where white-collar professionals, businessmen, and others facing career pressures could avoid social scrutiny.

At this point, Eric brought up Stonewall.

"Stonewall happened in New York in 1969. Pride came a year later. And then — everyone lived happily ever after."

He then turned serious.

"Obviously, that's not the case. Stonewall is important, but Stonewall was not the moment when history suddenly began. If all of these smaller events had not already been happening across the country, would Stonewall have had the same impact?"

Merced Theatre is still there, but the history presented on site does not show its intersection with LGBTQ history.

Pershing Square | Before the apps, the city was the network

Those social networks later extended around Pershing Square into an urban circuit known as "The Run," connecting the Biltmore Hotel, the Los Angeles Central Library, and nearby public and commercial spaces. Before mobile communication became widespread, the community relied on the regularity of place and time in these spaces to meet one another.

Eric described The Run as "kind of like RuPaul's Drag Race, gay Tinder, and cruising all at once," but he also stressed the vulnerability built into that system.

“You didn't even necessarily have to be LGBTQ.”

Police could use sting operations to accuse anyone of soliciting sex or attempting to seek sexual contact in public and arrest them.

Pershing Square itself later changed physically. In 1951, the park underwent major excavation and redesign for the construction of an underground parking garage, altering the original structure of the space.

The city did not need to demolish every building to change queer geography. Sometimes redesigning public space was enough to make earlier uses harder to recover.

Civic Center | Nancy Valverde and the law

At the Civic Center, the history shifted from social space to law and policing. In 1979, California Governor Jerry Brown appointed Stephen M. Lachs to the Los Angeles Superior Court. He later became known as the first openly gay judge appointed in the United States.

Not long before, LGBTQ people entering a courtroom were far more likely to be there because they had been arrested by police. Nancy Valverde, a Chicana barber, was among those repeatedly targeted. Because she often wore masculine clothing, she became a target of the Los Angeles Police Department under anti-masquerading enforcement.

Eric described how police would arrest her.

"Sometimes she was taken in on Friday and not released until Monday."

She later even had to explain to barber school that she was not late because she had spent the weekend having too much fun, but because she had just gotten out of jail.

According to The Guardian, Valverde told the historian Lillian Faderman that she went to the county law library in 1959 and found that the courts had decided in 1950 that a woman was not breaking the law simply for wearing masculine clothing. Valverde later recalled that she was able to use this legal precedent to stop the LAPD from arresting her, though officers continued to harass her.

Valverde later described that refusal to change in direct terms.

“They wanted me to be someone else. I could not be someone else. This is me.”

In June 2023, the intersection of Main and 2nd Streets was renamed Cooper Do-nuts / Nancy Valverde Square. According to ABC7, the dedication also came with a public apology from the Los Angeles Police Department. Commander Ruby Flores read a statement at the ceremony: "...this mistreatment and harassment of our citizens was wrong. It should have never happened." LAPD Police Chief Michel Moore also offered Valverde a personal apology by mobile phone.

Valverde died in 2024 at the age of 92. A person who had once been repeatedly arrested by the city because of what she wore eventually had her name written into the city's geography.

506 S Grand Ave. | The Biltmore Hotel and collective resistance

Another documented act of resistance took place at the Biltmore Hotel at 506 S Grand Ave. In 1970, the hotel was hosting a professional conference on behavior modification, which planned to screen a film about using "electroshock aversion therapy" to change homosexual orientation. Members of the Los Angeles Gay Liberation Front, including activist and librarian Carolyn Weathers, infiltrated the conference.

Eric emphasized how they entered.

"They didn't start out shouting. They didn't rush in and immediately create chaos. They were quiet. They spread out and sat in different places. What they needed to do was wait."

Only when the film was about to begin did they act collectively, interrupting the screening and then engaging the psychiatrists in dialogue. For the first time, a group of people who had long been observed, studied, classified, diagnosed, and treated by professional institutions forced those professionals to actually listen to them.

Three years later, in 1973, the American Psychiatric Association formally removed homosexuality from its manual of mental disorders.

The Biltmore still stands, but there is no sign on site telling people that this history happened here.

200 N Spring St. | City Hall and the nonlinearity of rights

Standing before Los Angeles City Hall at 200 N Spring St., Eric told the tour group:

“History isn't: the past was conservative, and then everything slowly became freer. It moves forward, and it moves backward.”

California's ballot history supports that point. In 1978, voters rejected Proposition 6, also known as the Briggs Initiative, which sought to allow public schools to dismiss teachers and other employees because of conduct or public advocacy related to homosexuality. Yet thirty years later, in 2008, the same state passed Proposition 8, writing a restriction on same-sex marriage into the state constitution and drawing crowds of protesters to Los Angeles City Hall.

Eric emphasized the reversal more than once.

"The same state."

The legal landscape shifted again five years later. At a 2014 symposium at Columbia Law School, Edith Windsor recalled that members of her legal team had urged her to stop after the lower-court ruling.

"They said, 'You can quit now,'" she said. "They told me the lower court ruling would stand and DOMA would be unconstitutional 'as applied to me.'"

Windsor refused.

"And I said, 'We're going to the Supreme Court,'" she said. "There was no way I could say to anyone else 'I won, and you lost' and call that a win."

In 2013, the Supreme Court's ruling in United States v. Windsor struck down the federal provision of DOMA that restricted recognition of marriage to one man and one woman.

Two days after the ruling, on June 28, 2013, Proposition 8 plaintiffs Paul Katami and Jeff Zarrillo were married inside Los Angeles City Hall, with outgoing Los Angeles Mayor Antonio Villaraigosa officiating.

The same building that had stood outside a protest in 2008 became a place of recognition in 2013.

Ending | What remains, and what does not

The tour concluded back at 232 S. Hill St. — the same empty lot where it had started.

This was once the Downtown Los Angeles office of ONE, Inc., the predecessor of One Institute, now the oldest active LGBTQ+ organization in the United States. Its official timeline shows that the organization was founded in November 1952, ONE Magazine debuted in January 1953, and it opened an office at 232 South Hill Street in November 1953. Los Angeles postal authorities seized the August 1953 and October 1954 issues of the magazine; after four years of litigation, the Supreme Court ruled on January 13, 1958, that the magazine did not violate obscenity laws.

When Eric first designed this walking tour, he tried to locate the original building.

"I remember thinking, 'Where is that building?'"

Only later did he realize it was no longer there. There is still no monument at the site, still no historic building, still no marker explaining why the address matters.

"Please share this history," Curry told the reporter and his brother. "I don't know how many people will still get the chance to learn about this history. I only know that every additional person I can tell means one more person will be able to see the buildings and histories that have disappeared."

Behind him, the lot sat empty, just as it had when we arrived. But after listening to Curry, it no longer looked like nothing. It was the site of ONE, Inc. — an office, a magazine, a legal battle, a Supreme Court ruling.

“The building was gone. The history was not.”
